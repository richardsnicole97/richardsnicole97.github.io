Current Work

CognitionStress experiences Neighborhood violence, sleep, and subjective cognition Can sleep help explain why adults exposed to more neighborhood violence rate their memory and thinking as worse? Manuscript in preparation · Accepted for GSA 2026
Design
Mailed survey on neighborhood violence, then a 14-day EMA with a morning sleep report and an evening rating of how sharp, fast, and memory-sound the day felt. Multilevel structural equation mediation model in Mplus.
Sample
249 adults ages 25–65 (average age 46.5; 65% women; 63% non-Hispanic Black, 24% Hispanic) living in Co-op City, Bronx, NY
Data
Effects of Stress on Cognitive Aging, Physiology and Emotion (ESCAPE) Project
Key finding
Adults who reported more neighborhood violence slept worse on average, and that poorer sleep accounted for their lower self-rated cognition. Day to day, nights of better-than-usual sleep were followed by sharper-feeling days, which points to sleep as a practical target for support.
  • Richards, N. E., Sherk, C., & Muñoz, E. (in preparation). Neighborhood violence and cognition.
  • Richards, N. E., Romañach Álvarez, L. S., Sherk, C., & Muñoz, E. (accepted). Neighborhood violence, sleep, and cognitive performance: Evidence from an ecological momentary assessment. GSA Annual Scientific Meeting, Baltimore, MD.
Stress experiencesDaily activityCognition Daily stress, activity contexts, and working memory in midlife Does a stressful moment get in the way of working memory, and does that depend on what you’re doing at the time? Under review · Presented at GSA 2025
Design
14-day smartphone EMA: three working memory tests a day (dot task), each followed by a survey on stress severity and current activity. Three-level multilevel models (moments within people within families).
Sample
440 adults ages 28–51 (average age 36; 58% women), 4,334 assessments
Data
Colorado Adoption/Twin Study of Lifespan (CATSLife)
Key finding
Stress in the moment didn’t predict working memory on its own, but context did. For women, high stress while “doing nothing special” went with worse working memory, while socializing buffered that link. Men showed neither pattern.
  • Richards, N. E., Reynolds, C. A., Zheng, A., Wadsworth, S. J., Sliwinski, M. J., & Muñoz, E. (under review). Here and now: Daily stress, activity contexts, and cognitive performance in midlife. Health Psychology.
  • Richards, N. E., Muñoz, E., Zheng, A., Wadsworth, S. J., Sliwinski, M. J., & Reynolds, C. A. (2025, November). Daily cognitive performance under stress: Contextual and gender differences. GSA Annual Scientific Meeting, Boston, MA.
Personal relationships Gratitude, relationship qualities, and life satisfaction in late life Does gratitude help older adults stay satisfied with life when their close relationships are less warm or more tense? Presented at GSA 2023
Design
Survey of trait gratitude and life satisfaction, plus ratings of affection and conflict with each of up to 10 closest social partners. Multiple regression with moderation.
Sample
316 community-dwelling adults ages 65–92 (average age 74) in the greater Austin area
Data
Daily Experiences and Well-being Study (DEWS)
Key finding
More affection in close ties went with higher life satisfaction. Gratitude acted as a buffer: less grateful older adults were less satisfied when their relationships were low in affection, while highly grateful adults stayed satisfied either way. Gratitude helped less when conflict was high.
  • Richards, N. E., Zhou, Z., & Fingerman, K. L. (2023, November). Gratitude, relationship qualities, and life satisfaction in late life. GSA Annual Scientific Meeting, Tampa, FL.
